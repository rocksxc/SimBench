**Summary**

The paper asks a clean design question: when a population functional Δ(P) = E[Y d(X)] (the accuracy of a frozen classifier, or the accuracy gain d = p1 − p0 of a model update) must be estimated from records that each reveal at most b of m binary features, how many more records does a collector need if it pre-commits to a feature set per record (fixed forms, one round) instead of choosing each question after seeing earlier answers (branching, a depth-b decision tree), with r-round batched interviews in between? Labels are observed for every record and charged separately.

Theorem 1 shows that for any finite menu of interviews the first-order minimax MSE constant is a finite convex program (minimum worst-profile second moment of a pointwise-unbiased Horvitz–Thompson-type score), with a dual in terms of the largest projection energy any single interview can capture; the converse holds against biased estimators, cross-record adaptation and label-dependent interview choice, and a finite-sample version holds within a (large, acknowledged) factor 10^4. Theorem 2 gives the sharp worst-case ratio V_fixed/V_branch ≤ min{2^{b−1}, C(m−1,b−1)}, attained by an address contrast, with m/b under a mean rather than hard budget. Theorem 3 bounds the loss of r rounds by 2^{b−r} and separates r from r+1 rounds by 2^{⌊(b−1)/r⌋}/b via pointer-chasing contrasts. Theorem 4 brackets the population-specific fixed/branching second-moment ratio between 1/Γ_P (inverse of the energy explained by the best single form) and N_P(τ) (Rényi-1/2 effective number of the tree's path sets). A column-generation algorithm with an O(m^b 2^b) dynamic-programming pricing step computes the exact branching constant over all depth-b trees (≈1.5×10^7 trees at m=12, b=3) with certified bounds.

Experiments compute these constants exactly on (i) 180 "binding" depth-3 random-forest updates on WDBC/Spambase/Ionosphere with 6–12 median-binarized features (best fixed design needs median 1.51×, max 1.85× the records; menus restricted to the forests' own trees capture only ~17% of the log-gain), (ii) depth 3–6 CART screening trees on BRFSS diabetes items (1.35–1.58×, max 1.98×) and thyroid (1.0×, as predicted by Γ_P = 1), with naive path guessing up to 25×, and partial recovery of the gap by extra rounds; (iii) a simulation showing multiple imputation from complete source records matches branching when the conditional law transports (RMSE 0.88–1.00×) while design-based fixed-form scores cost 1.00–1.22×; and (iv) a PISA 2012 planned-missing archive illustrating identification from real fixed forms.

**Strengths**

1. A genuinely natural, precisely formulated question that sits between planned-missingness survey design, active feature acquisition and query-complexity adaptivity, and that I have not seen answered in statistical (sample-count) rather than query-count terms. Converting the classical "adaptive vs. non-adaptive decision tree" separation into an exact first-order minimax constant under a per-record cap is a nice idea.
2. The theory is correct as far as I checked and the proofs are careful. The path-guessing simulation (A12.1; completion iff S = S_τ(x), L_τ ≤ 2^{b−1} because the first b−1 answers determine the path's coordinate set), the address lower bound via projection energy r2^{s−2κ} ≤ 2^{−κ} (A12.3), the Fenchel/Sion duality and van Trees converse (A11), the pointer-chasing "hit" argument (A3, Steps 1–3) and the Cauchy–Schwarz/Rényi bracket (A4) all hold up. The converse covers biased estimators, cross-record adaptation and label-dependent interview choice, which is the right generality for an evaluation audience.
3. The exact computation of the branching constant over all depth-3 trees by certified column generation is a real methodological contribution: convergence on all 240 tasks to relative gap ≤ 9×10^{-7}, an independent reimplementation reproducing every ratio to three decimals, and fitted designs (optimized on training records, evaluated held-out) that realize 1.67× vs. 1.64× oracle. This is unusually rigorous empirical work for a theory paper.
4. The population-specific bracket (Theorem 4) is interpretable and useful as a pre-collection diagnostic; the thyroid result (Γ_P = 1 ⇒ no gain) and the finding that "restricted branching menus miss most of the gain" are genuinely informative for practitioners.
5. Honest framing of limitations: order/mode effects (A24), identification floors for thresholded/nonlinear models (A26), the cost regime where complete records beat any capped design (A31), and the imputation comparison that partially undercuts the headline. Reproducibility (code, certificates, release checks) appears thorough.

**Weaknesses**

1. [major] Limited reach for the ML audience. Identifiability requires Fourier degree ≤ b, which holds for probability-averaged depth-≤b tree ensembles and single trees but fails for thresholded forests, GBDT with a logistic link, and MLPs (the paper certifies floors in A26 rather than estimating). Labels are assumed free and observed for every record, whereas in most ML evaluation settings (active testing, PPI) label cost dominates—the paper's own A28.2/A31 show complete records are often cheaper once labels cost ≥ ~3 feature units. The "budgeted classifier" motivation in the abstract is never exercised: no experiment evaluates an AFA or cost-sensitive policy. In practice the setting is survey screening and clinical test ordering with cheap labels, which is fine but narrower than the framing.
2. [major] Modest realized gains and the imputation caveat. On every real task the fixed/branching factor is 1.0–1.7 (max 1.98); the exponential 2^{b−1} is attained only by contrived address contrasts. When complete source records exist and the conditional law transports, fixed forms + MI already match or beat branching (RMSE 0.88–1.00×, Table 4). So the operational message is "within-record adaptivity saves 0–40% of records for design-based inference on small trees/forests", which is honest but considerably less compelling than the abstract suggests.
3. [major] Scale and the meaning of "population" in the exact computations. All experiments use m ≤ 12 median-binarized features, 20-tree depth-3 forests, and b ≤ 6. The fixed-form SOCP has O(C(m,b) 2^b) variables and the column-generation pricing is O(m^b 2^b); neither the main text nor the appendix discusses what happens at realistic survey/clinical sizes (dozens of ordinal items, b = 8–10). The "population" is the held-out empirical law (e.g., 171 Ionosphere records over 4096 profiles) mixed with 1% uniform mass; the local-minimax interpretation (Prop. 22) only holds for this artificial full-support law, and no sensitivity to the smoothing mass or to the held-out sample is reported for the new experiments (A23.3 covers a different floor parameter on the earlier SUSHI/SAPA tasks).
4. [major] Presentation and self-containedness. The main text is precise but extremely dense for ICML: it presupposes Boolean Fourier analysis, van Trees, coarsening-at-random augmentation and column generation, and the three key constructions (address contrast, path guessing, pointer chasing) have no illustration (Figure 1 is small and generic). The 48-page appendix A1–A32 is not in citation order, mixes current results with "earlier experiments of the previous version" (A8, A22–A23, A28, A30.3) on datasets (SUSHI, SAPA) and tables (6, 9, 11, 21) never introduced in the paper, and repeatedly refers to "the earlier release/analysis". A reader cannot easily tell which appendix material supports the submitted claims.
5. [minor] Theorem 4 brackets the second-moment ratio M_fixed/M_branch, while Section 6 reports variance ratios W_fixed/W_branch. Since W_f/W_b ≥ M_f/M_b when the latter exceeds one, the lower end 1/Γ_P transfers but the upper end N_P does not directly; "every run lies inside the bracket" (p. 5) should be stated precisely (it is comfortably true numerically here).
6. [minor] The "Two"/r-round columns (Table 2–3) use a specific heuristic (follow the tree for r−1 questions, then the best fixed form in the subcube), not the optimal r-round design; "recovers 33–63% of the log-gap" is therefore a lower bound on what r rounds can achieve and should be labeled as such.
7. [minor] Depth of the theoretical novelty. The address function and pointer chasing are the classical separating examples for adaptive vs. non-adaptive decision trees and round hierarchies; path guessing is a Horvitz–Thompson estimator; q_S ∝ √w_S is the standard importance-sampling optimum. The contribution is the exact statistical constants and the uniform treatment over adaptive menus, which is nice but of moderate depth; the finite-sample constant 10^4 is far from tight, and the exponent for 2 ≤ r < b and the exact (m,b) frontier (even at m=4, b=3: between 2.457 and 3) are open.
8. [minor] A24 shows that position-dependent answer errors of 10% can induce bias of 0.5–1.8 branching standard errors at n = 1000, i.e., comparable to or larger than the sampling gain. Given that the paper's practical regime is surveys, this deserves a sentence with numbers in the main text, not only an appendix pointer.

**Soundness** (3): The theorems I checked (1–4, A3, A11, A12, A20.1) are correct and carefully proved, and the exact computations are certified and independently reimplemented; remaining concerns are about what the smoothed sparse "population" represents and the M-vs-W wording, not correctness.

**Presentation** (2): Well-structured and precise main text, but very dense and not self-contained for an ML audience, with a sprawling appendix that is out of citation order and carries legacy experiments and datasets never introduced in the paper.

**Significance** (3): A sharp answer to a natural design question plus a usable exact-computation tool and a pre-collection diagnostic; but realized gains are modest (1.0–1.7×), the identifiability scope excludes most deployed ML models, and labels are assumed free, so the impact on ML evaluation practice is moderate.

**Originality** (3): The formulation (statistical price of within-record non-adaptivity under a per-record cap) and the exact constants are new; the separating examples, estimators and duality tools are classical, recombined with care.

**Key Questions for Authors**

1. Scalability: how do the fixed-form SOCP over all C(m,b) forms and the O(m^b 2^b) pricing behave at realistic sizes (m = 30–60 ordinal items, b = 6–10)? Is there a certified approximate route (e.g., restricting to path-set menus as in A7 with a dual bound over all forms)? A credible path to realistic scale would raise my significance assessment; if the exact machinery is intrinsically limited to m ≈ 12, I would weight the paper as a small-scale theory contribution.
2. Sensitivity of the "exact" ratios in Tables 1–2 to the 1% uniform smoothing (e.g., 0.1%, 1%, 10%) and to resampling the held-out set (bootstrap over the 171–3,101 evaluation records). Stable medians would remove my soundness reservation about what population these constants describe; large swings would lower it.
3. Decision relevance of the bracket: you report correlation 0.44 between Γ_branch/Γ_fixed and the exact ratio on forests, and the bracket contains every screening run. Can 1/Γ_P computed from reference data alone predict the realized W-ratio well enough to decide, before collection, whether skip logic is worth its operational cost? A validated rule (e.g., prediction error of the realized ratio across all 240 + 48 tasks) would increase practical significance substantially.
4. Label-cost regime: A28.2 gives the fixed-vs-complete crossover. For an ML evaluation reader, what is the branching-vs-complete and branching-vs-(fixed + MI) comparison as a function of label cost c_Y and of the availability of complete source records? If branching retains an advantage over a meaningful range, the ML framing becomes credible; if it only wins at c_Y ≈ 0 without a source sample, the paper should be framed squarely as survey/clinical design.
5. Theorem 1 says cross-record adaptation does not improve the worst-case first-order constant. Population-specifically, can an across-record adaptive design that learns q (and tables) from earlier records attain W_A(P) without a reference sample, and at what rate? A positive answer connects directly to active testing/active inference and would raise my view of relevance; a negative or unknown answer leaves the fitted-design results (3–7% loss) as the practical story.

**Limitations**

Yes—Section 8, the Impact Statement, and Appendices A24, A26, A31 discuss order/mode effects, identifiability floors, the full-label assumption, dependence on reference data, and cost regimes candidly. I would add to the main text (i) the quantitative A24 finding that modest position effects can exceed the sampling gain, and (ii) the computational scaling limits of the exact programs.

**Overall Recommendation**: 4 — Weak accept. A technically solid and honest paper with a crisp new characterization and an impressive exact computation, whose practical significance for the ICML audience is moderate and whose presentation will lose many readers.

**Confidence**: 4

**What would move you up one point**

- An end-to-end demonstration at realistic scale (dozens of items, ordinal answers, depth 6–10 trees) with a scalable, certified computation, showing at least one real setting where the design-based gain is large (≥2×) and predicted in advance by 1/Γ_P.
- A finite-sample study on an ML-relevant target (e.g., validating a budgeted/AFA classifier or a model update) against strong model-based and doubly-robust baselines, including the label-cost axis, showing the adaptivity gain translates into decision-relevant savings in a regime where ML practitioners actually operate.
- A substantially streamlined, self-contained presentation: a main-body figure illustrating the address contrast, path guessing and pointer chasing; the appendix reordered to match citations and pruned of (or clearly quarantined from) the earlier-version SUSHI/SAPA material.
- Closing one of the open theoretical ends (the exponent for 2 ≤ r < b rounds, or the exact (m,b) frontier) would also strengthen the paper, though the first two items matter more for my assessment.
